# Long-Form Continuity

Load this reference for long-running fiction, a high-risk rewrite, or an explicit continuity audit. It is backstage procedure, not a prose template.

## Resolve Source Validity

For each source needed by the target chapter, note its coverage, status (`active`, `candidate`, `historical/stale`, or `mixed/unverified`), and why that status applies. A self-declared historical snapshot is not current authority merely because its filename says “ledger.” If later logs were appended beneath a stale header, use those entries as retrieval leads and verify the relevant claims against accepted prose.

Retrieve only spans relevant to the target, plus adjacent chapters when the boundary matters. Separate:

- what the author knows from the whole manuscript;
- what the viewpoint character knows at this point;
- what the reader has learned by this point.

Do not leak later revelations into earlier character knowledge. A plan is not evidence that a scene occurred.

## Maintain a Minimal Moving Ledger

Use existing state documents where practical. If none exist and work crosses chapters, create one short current snapshot plus per-chapter candidate deltas. Backfill only the characters and facts needed for the active arc.

| Track | Record when it changes |
| --- | --- |
| Character | location, injury or limitation, usable resources, active aim, stable speech or behavioral boundary |
| Relationship | trust, debt, power, promises, boundary, and the action that changed it |
| Knowledge | fact identifier; each person's level `unknown`, `suspects`, or `knows`; how they learned it |
| Object and money | holder, condition, expenditure, transfer, or loss |
| Time and clue | story-day or relative order; planted, advanced, resolved, or still open |
| Chapter promise | what the ending asks the next chapter to pay and whether it does |

Attach a source anchor, effective chapter range, last verification point, and status to each nontrivial fact. Keep inference marked as inference even when the author or reader knows the answer. A list of personality adjectives is not a state ledger.

## Prepare the Chapter Brief

Select only facts that change the scene's action, cost, perception, or misunderstanding. Include exact geometry and material for a central object, current physical limits, live obligations, relevant names, and adjacent-chapter boundaries. Keep provenance in a separate evidence map so the author can write naturally.

When a source is incomplete, mark the fact `unknown` in the backstage brief. Characters may wonder or suspect; narration must not silently establish an answer. A new detail that constrains future chapters remains proposed, not canon.

## Review the Draft

Compare the candidate with its source anchors and preceding accepted prose. Check names and aliases, time, physical state, relationship changes, knowledge source, prop holder, paid cost, and clue or promise continuity.

For each finding, record:

`claim in draft | source anchor | pass / conflict / uncertain | proposed action`

Revise conflicts against evidence. Leave uncertain facts visible instead of inventing a fix. Literary editing may change rhythm and dialogue but must not silently change facts. Recheck revised lines. Merge new facts only after the user approves the chapter and its candidate delta.

For a consequential chapter, also check whether:

- an irreversible loss or limitation was quietly rolled back without a recovery chain;
- a relationship or trust step changed without an observable trigger;
- a major choice created the expected material, social, legal, or political consequence;
- a character acted on information they had not learned;
- a new term or explanation displaced the scene's immediate action.

Use project-specific constraints only when their scope and current status are clear. A numerical rule that conflicts with a chapter's causality should be surfaced for review, not hidden by rewriting the scene around the metric.

## Common Failure Patterns

- A status document's header and appended chapter logs disagree; neither should be accepted wholesale without checking the relevant section.
- A compressed brief preserves “three restraints” but omits which limbs are bound and what the fastener is made of; fluent prose can still contradict the scene.
- A checker passes an incorrect value that was copied into canon; accepted prose remains an important evidence source.
- A chapter promises an immediate reveal, but the next accepted chapter skips it.
- An author's knowledge is mistakenly assigned to a viewpoint character who has not learned the fact.
